1. The big idea

An embedding is a list of numbers (a vector) that stands for a token. In your runs each token gets 32 numbers. On their own the numbers mean nothing, but they are arranged so that tokens used in similar ways end up with vectors pointing in similar directions.

Skip-gram learns those vectors from one simple observation, often summed up as "you shall know a word by the company it keeps":

The model never sees a definition of any word. It only plays a guessing game, millions of times over: "Here are two tokens. Did they really appear near each other in the text, or is this a random pairing?" To get good at that game it has to place tokens with similar neighbours close together. The vectors that result are the embeddings.

Why is "mat" split, but "cat" isn't?

In the codetrain_bpe merge loopbpe.py lines 261–282stop if best pair occurs < 2 timesbpe.py lines 271–272merges applied in learned orderbpe.py lines 224–225

It comes down to how often each word appears in the text the tokenizer learned from. train_bpe starts with every word split into single letters. Then, step by step, it finds the most frequent pair of neighbouring tokens in the whole corpus and merges that pair into a new token. Pairs that occur often get merged; rare ones don't.

MergePairTimes the pair occurred when merged
#6a + t → at29 (cat, sat, at, mat, cats, …)
#18c + at → cat13 (cat × 11, cats × 2)
neverm + at → mat1 (mat × 1)

The stopping rule. train_bpe stops as soon as the most frequent remaining pair occurs fewer than 2 times, because merging a pair that appears only once doesn't help compress the text. That rule is what ended training on your corpus: after 261 merges (out of the 500 allowed), the most frequent pair left was m + at, with a count of 1. "mat" is literally the last word left unmerged.

A subtlety. The tokenizer did learn m + a → ma (merge #132, from words like "man" and "woman"). Yet "mat" still comes out as m + at, not ma + t. That's because split_word applies the merges in the order they were learned. a + t (#6) runs long before m + a (#132), so by the time ma could apply, the a is already part of at.

Why it matters here: "mat" has no embedding of its own. Training sees m and at as two separate tokens, and the at vector is shared with the word "at" and with every other word split around it. It's also why find_query_indices warns about query words like this.

6. Step 4: Scoring a pair

In the codeEmbeddingVector.dotembed.py lines 309–318sigmoidembed.py lines 358–365the ±6 shortcutembed.py lines 361–364score in train_pairembed.py line 553

For a pair (target, other), the model needs a guess: how likely is it that these two really appeared together? It works that out in two steps.

a) The dot product: do the vectors agree?

Multiply the two vectors element by element and add up the results. This is EmbeddingVector.dot:

dot = t[0]×o[0] + t[1]×o[1] + … + t[31]×o[31]
EmbeddingVector.dotembed.py lines 314–318

b) The sigmoid: turn agreement into a probability

A dot product can be any number. sigmoid squashes it into the range 0 to 1, so it can be read as a probability:

score = sigmoid(dot) = 1 / (1 + e−dot)
sigmoidembed.py lines 360–365

At the start every vector is tiny, so every dot product is about 0 and every score is about 0.5. The model starts out guessing "no idea" for everything.

7. Step 5: Negative samples

In the codenegative loop in train_epochembed.py lines 581–586skip if neg == contextembed.py lines 584–585build_negative_sampling_tableembed.py lines 387–439frequency ** 0.75embed.py line 430running totalembed.py lines 436–438sample_negativeembed.py lines 441–463table built in train_skip_gramembed.py line 656

If the model only ever saw real pairs and was told "yes" each time, it would find a lazy solution: make every vector huge and pointing the same way, so every score is 1. It would be 100% right and would have learned nothing.

So for every real (positive) pair, train_epoch also makes up 5 fake pairs (negative_samples = 5): the same target, paired with tokens picked at random. For those the right answer is "no".

Which random tokens? The negative-sampling table

Negatives aren't picked uniformly. build_negative_sampling_table gives each token a probability based on its frequency raised to the power 0.75 (a trick from the original word2vec paper). Common tokens are still picked more often, but the 0.75 flattens things out so rare tokens get more of a chance than their raw count would give them:

To actually pick one, the table stores a running total. Each token owns a stretch of the line from 0 to 1 as wide as its probability. sample_negative draws a random number r and finds whose stretch it lands in (using a binary search):

8. Step 6: The nudge (train_pair)

In the codetrain_pairembed.py lines 524–562EmbeddingVector.add_scaledembed.py lines 320–324EmbeddingVector.copyembed.py lines 326–328

This is where learning actually happens. train_pair is called once for each positive pair and once for each negative, about 15,000 times per epoch. Here's the whole thing:

def train_pair(self, w_in, w_out, target, other, label, lr):
    target_vector = w_in[target]
    other_vector = w_out[other]

    score = self.sigmoid(target_vector.dot(other_vector))   # 1. guess
    grad = lr * (label - score)                            # 2. how wrong?

    old_target_vector = target_vector.copy()
    target_vector.add_scaled(other_vector, grad)            # 3. nudge both vectors
    other_vector.add_scaled(old_target_vector, grad)

    if label == 1:
        return -math.log(score + 1e-10)                     # 4. report the loss
    return -math.log(1 - score + 1e-10)

The key line: grad = lr * (label - score)

label − score is the error: the right answer minus the guess.

Situationlabelscorelabel − scoreEffect
Real pair, guessed "no idea"10.5+0.5pull together firmly
Real pair, already confident10.95+0.05pull together a little
Fake pair, guessed "no idea"00.5−0.5push apart firmly
Fake pair, already confident00.05−0.05push apart a little

So the size of the nudge depends on how wrong the guess was, and its sign decides the direction. lr, the learning rate, scales everything down so each nudge is tiny.

What "nudge" means geometrically

target_vector.add_scaled(other_vector, grad) adds grad × other_vector to the target. If grad is positive, the target moves a little towards the other vector; if negative, a little away from it. The other vector gets the mirror-image update. That's why a copy of the target is taken first: both updates must use the values from before either one changed.

9. What is "loss"?

In the codeloss in train_pairembed.py lines 560–562summed in train_epochembed.py lines 577–586averaged per pairembed.py line 588reported as EpochResultembed.py lines 673–674round_6embed.py lines 590–593

Loss is a number that measures how wrong the model's guesses are. 0 means perfect; bigger means worse. Training never uses loss to decide anything. It's a report card, so you can watch the model improve.

For a single pair, train_pair returns:

  • for a real pair: −log(score), which is small when the score is near 1
  • for a fake pair: −log(1 − score), which is small when the score is near 0

Why the logarithm? It's the standard way to measure probability guesses (called log loss or cross-entropy). It's gentle when you're nearly right and severe when you're confidently wrong. The + 1e-10 just stops log(0), which would be infinite.

The loss that train_skip_gram reports

train_epoch adds up the loss of every train_pair call in the epoch (one positive and up to five negatives per pair) and divides by the number of pairs. So the reported loss is roughly the total loss of six guesses.

Why the first loss is about 4.12: at the start every score is about 0.5, so every guess has a loss of about 0.693. Six guesses per pair gives 6 × 0.693 ≈ 4.16. The reported 4.124 is just under that because a few negatives get skipped, and the model already improves a little during that first epoch.

Here's the real loss from a 100-epoch run (vector size 32, window 2, 5 negatives, seed 42):

The loss never gets to 0, and it can't. A token like "the" appears next to almost everything, so some "fake" pairs are perfectly plausible, and the model can't score them all perfectly. Loss tells you whether training is working, not how good the embeddings are. For that, look at neighbours, similarities and analogies.

Watching similarities develop

Another way to watch training is to track cosine similarity, which measures how closely two vectors point the same way (1 = same direction, 0 = unrelated, −1 = opposite) and ignores their lengths. Here are five pairs through the whole run:

Why does everything become similar at first?

That middle phase surprises most people. Here is what's going on.

  • The learning rate is at its highest and every guess is maximally wrong, so the first epochs make big updates.
  • Every token shares a lot of the same company: almost every sentence has "the", "a" and common letter tokens around. So early on, every target vector gets pulled in roughly the same direction, towards "a token that appears in this corpus".
  • That shared direction quickly dominates. The average vector length jumps from 0.03 to 1.42 by epoch 5. When every vector is mostly the same big shared component plus small individual differences, they all point nearly the same way, so cosine similarity is close to 1 for every pair.
  • Small individual differences remain underneath. The scatter plots remove the shared component so you can see them. At epoch 5 the main split is between common and rarer words (kitten, puppy, lion, tiger, pizza and food on one side), which is about frequency, not meaning. Over the following epochs the differences grow, driven by the specific company each word keeps and by the negative samples pushing unrelated tokens apart, and the meaning-based groups appear.

Summary of refinement: each pair nudges two vectors a tiny amount. Nudges for words with similar company point in similar directions, so they add up, while random noise cancels out. Over hundreds of thousands of nudges the vectors grow longer and their directions settle into a layout where meaning shows up as geometry.

12. The role of randomness

Why each one is needed

1. Random starting weights break the symmetry. Suppose every vector started at all zeros. Then every dot product is 0, every score is 0.5, and every nudge is grad × other_vector = grad × [0, 0, …] = nothing. The vectors would stay at zero forever. Even if they started equal but non-zero, tokens with the same starting point would get very similar updates. Small random values give every token a slightly different starting point to grow from. They're kept small so that no token starts with a big built-in bias.

2. Shuffling avoids order effects. Your corpus is grouped by topic (pets, then royalty, and so on). Without shuffling, each epoch would end with the same topic, and the final nudges of every epoch would always favour it. Shuffling gives every pair a fair, varying position.

3. Random negatives are a cheap stand-in for "everything else". Ideally, each real pair would push the target away from all 285 other tokens. That would be about 50 times more work. Sampling 5 at random, weighted by frequency, gives about the same effect on average for far less work. That's the "negative sampling" in "skip-gram with negative sampling".

Seeded, so it's repeatable

mulberry32 is a pseudo-random generator: a short formula that turns a starting number (the seed) into a sequence that looks random but is completely determined by the seed.

That's why running your code twice gives identical results to the last digit, and why the Python matches the TypeScript: both use the same generator with the same seed, and draw from it in exactly the same order. (That's also why shuffle uses rand() instead of Python's random.shuffle, and why init_weights alternates between w_in and w_out value by value.)

One stream, so everything is connected. Because there's only one generator, changing anything that uses random numbers changes everything after it. For example, with negative_samples = 4 instead of 5, the second epoch's shuffle uses different random numbers, so the order is different, so every later nudge is different.

The seed is fixed at 42 in train_skip_gram. Here's what the same 100-epoch training gives with seeds 1, 2 and 3 instead:

The big picture is stable: king/queen and man/woman are always very similar, man/pizza is always low, and the easy analogies always work. The details vary, and borderline cases flip. "his − man + woman" finds her with seeds 1 and 2, but not with 3 or 42. Once the loop finishes, w_in holds the embeddings, and the rest of train_skip_gram reports on the query words you passed in opts.words:

  • embeddings: each query word's vector, rounded to 6 decimal places (get_vector).
  • neighbors: for each query word, the 5 tokens in the whole vocabulary with the highest cosine similarity.
  • similarities: cosine similarity for every pair of query words.
  • analogies: vector arithmetic, shown below.
  • warnings: query words that aren't a single token, so have no vector of their own (find_query_indices).

Analogies: arithmetic on meaning

If the vectors have captured meaning, then the difference between two vectors should capture a relationship. king − man is roughly "royalty without the male part"; adding woman should land near "queen":

With your small corpus some analogies work (king → queen, queen → king, prince → princess, he → she) and some don't (kitten − cat + dog gives tiger instead of puppy). Analogies are a strict test: the answer must be the single closest of 286 tokens, so a small amount of noise can make the wrong one win.
