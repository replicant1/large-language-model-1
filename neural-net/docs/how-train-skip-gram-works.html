1. The big idea

An embedding is a list of numbers (a vector) that stands for a token. In your runs each token gets 32 numbers. On their own the numbers mean nothing, but they are arranged so that tokens used in similar ways end up with vectors pointing in similar directions.

Skip-gram learns those vectors from one simple observation, often summed up as "you shall know a word by the company it keeps":

The model never sees a definition of any word. It only plays a guessing game, millions of times over: "Here are two tokens. Did they really appear near each other in the text, or is this a random pairing?" To get good at that game it has to place tokens with similar neighbours close together. The vectors that result are the embeddings.

Why is "mat" split, but "cat" isn't?

It comes down to how often each word appears in the text the tokenizer learned from. train_bpe starts with every word split into single letters. Then, step by step, it finds the most frequent pair of neighbouring tokens in the whole corpus and merges that pair into a new token. Pairs that occur often get merged; rare ones don't.

MergePairTimes the pair occurred when merged
#6a + t → at29 (cat, sat, at, mat, cats, …)
#18c + at → cat13 (cat × 11, cats × 2)
neverm + at → mat1 (mat × 1)

The stopping rule. train_bpe stops as soon as the most frequent remaining pair occurs fewer than 2 times. That rule is what ended training on your corpus: after 261 merges (out of the 500 allowed), the most frequent pair left was m + at, with a count of 1. It wasn't special, though: all 162 pairs still left occurred exactly once, and m + at was simply the first of them that max() came across.

Why stop at pairs that occur only once? They don't compress anything. BPE's goal is to find pieces of text that occur repeatedly, so the corpus can be written with fewer, larger tokens. Merging c + at pays off 13 times, saving a token in every "cat" and "cats". Merging m + at would pay off once, saving a single token in the whole corpus. It would add a vocabulary entry just to memorise one word. Real tokenizers do the same: Sennrich's subword-nmt, the reference implementation from the paper that brought BPE to NLP, stops by default when no pair occurs at least twice.

A subtlety. The tokenizer did learn m + a → ma (merge #132, from words like "man" and "woman"). Yet "mat" still comes out as m + at, not ma + t. That's because split_word applies the merges in the order they were learned. a + t (#6) runs long before m + a (#132), so by the time ma could apply, the a is already part of at.

Why it matters here: "mat" has no embedding of its own. Training sees m and at as two separate tokens, and the at vector is shared with the word "at" and with every other word split around it. It's also why find_query_indices warns about query words like this.

5. Step 3: Two tables of vectors

In the codeinitialize_weightsskip_gram_network.py lines 63–80scale = 0.5 / dimskip_gram_network.py line 74random weights, alternating w_in / w_outskip_gram_network.py lines 79–80called from train_skip_gramembed.py line 558embedding / context_vectorskip_gram_network.py lines 47–53

The model is a small neural network, a SkipGramNetwork built by init_network. It has one input neuron per token, 32 hidden neurons, and one output neuron per token, joined by synapses that each carry a weight. Those weights fall into two sets, which this page calls w_in and w_out (the standard word2vec names, explained below). Each set can be pictured as a table with one row per token (286 rows) and one column per dimension (32 columns):

Where the two tables live in the code. There are no table objects: every number is the weight on one synapse.

  • w_in[t] is the weights on the 32 synapses going out of token t's input neuron, one to each hidden neuron. network.embedding(t) reads them.
  • w_out[c] is the weights on the 32 synapses coming into token c's output neuron, one from each hidden neuron. network.context_vector(c) reads them.

So a token's embedding is, quite literally, the weights on its input neuron's outgoing synapses.

The names come from word2vec's original description as a small neural network. The target word goes in at one end, and a prediction about its context comes out at the other:

So "in" and "out" say where the weights sit in the network: on the input side, where the target goes in, and on the output side, where the context prediction comes out. (The original paper calls them each word's "input vector" and "output vector"; the original C code calls them syn0 and syn1.)

"Target/context" and "in/out" are the same split seen from two angles: the target enters on the input side, and the context is predicted on the output side. That's why w_in holds target vectors and w_out holds context vectors.

Target vs context: two roles, two vectors

In the codeindices[i] is the target, indices[j] the contextembed.py lines 345–347target uses w_in, context uses w_outskip_gram_network.py lines 123–124negatives replace the contextembed.py lines 486–489only w_in is keptembed.py lines 511–513

"Target" and "context" are roles in a pair, not kinds of token. The target is the token at the centre of the window, the one being asked about. The context is one of its neighbours within window_size. Each pair asks: "Given this target, is this a token that appears around it?" That's where the name comes from: skip-gram predicts the surrounding words from the centre word. (Its sister method, CBOW, does the reverse.)

The linguistic rationale: words relate in two ways

In the codew_in[w] · w_out[c]: the training scoreskip_gram_network.py line 92w_in[w] vs w_in[other words]: neighboursembed.py lines 583–597w_in[w1] vs w_in[w2]: similaritiesembed.py lines 599–608

Why give every token two vectors in the first place? Because words relate to each other in two different ways, and the two vectors give the model a separate place to learn each one. Linguists have distinguished these two relations since Ferdinand de Saussure, over a century ago:

RelationQuestion it answersExampleLearned by
Syntagmatic (combination)Which words go next to this word?king → crowned, was, becamew_in[king] · w_out[crowned]
Paradigmatic (substitution)Which words could replace this word?king → queen, princew_in[king] · w_in[queen]

Why compare two vectors instead of reading one? A single vector such as w_in[king] is 32 numbers, none of which means anything by itself: there's no "royalty" or "gender" position to read. Training only ever uses dot products between vectors, and rotating every vector in both tables by the same amount leaves all dot products unchanged, so the individual numbers are arbitrary. Only the angles and lengths between vectors are fixed by training, just as a city's map coordinates depend on how the map is drawn but the distance between two cities doesn't. So every question about words compares two of them, and each · above is a dot product between two different words' vectors, never a vector with itself.

Crucially, the two relations point to different words. The words that are similar to "cat" (dog, mouse) are mostly not the words that appear next to "cat" (the, sat, chased). A word is almost never next to its substitutes (you don't say "the cat dog sat"), and almost never a substitute for its neighbours ("the" can't replace "cat").

Why that forces two vectors

In the codethe network: two sets of synapsesskip_gram_network.py lines 33–39filled with separate random valuesskip_gram_network.py lines 79–80target from w_in, context from w_outskip_gram_network.py lines 123–124

Suppose each token had only one vector, and the score were sigmoid(v_cat · v_x). Training would have to make v_cat · v_the large, because "the" appears next to "cat" constantly. But that same dot product is also what we'd use to measure similarity, so "cat" would come out similar to "the". A single vector per word can't keep "goes next to" separate from "is like". It mixes the two relations up.

With two tables, the jobs are split:

  • w_in[w] · w_out[c], one word's target vector against another word's context vector, learns the syntagmatic relation. It's the training signal: "does x appear next to this word?"
  • w_in[w1] · w_in[w2], the target vectors of two different words, gives the paradigmatic relation for free. Two words whose target vectors are pulled towards the same context vectors end up alike.

The deeper root: the distributional hypothesis

In the codeco-occurrence: pairs from CORPUSembed.py lines 545–546the window that defines a neighbourembed.py lines 345–347input-side (w_in) and output-side (w_out) synapsesskip_gram_network.py lines 73–80

Why train on the syntagmatic relation at all, when the paradigmatic one is what we want? Because one can be measured from text and the other can't, and the first turns out to reveal the second.

  • Co-occurrence can be counted. "Does crowned appear within 2 tokens of king?" is a question you can answer by scanning the sentences and counting. The answer is written in the text.
  • Substitutability can't be counted. "Could queen replace king and the sentence still make sense?" is a question about sentences that might exist, not ones that do. No sentence in the corpus says "king and queen are interchangeable". You'd have to try swapping words and judge whether the result is natural, which needs a speaker of the language.

The bridge between them is the distributional hypothesis (Harris, 1954; Firth, 1957: "you shall know a word by the company it keeps"). Words that could replace each other tend to turn up with the same neighbours. So you can estimate the thing you can't count by comparing the thing you can: list each word's neighbours, then see how much the lists overlap. Here are the real neighbours (window 2) in your CORPUS:

WordSome of its neighboursShared with king
kingwas, crowned, became, become, will, sits, wore, …
queenwas, crowned, became, become, will, sits, wore, …7 meaningful words, plus common ones like "the" and "of"
pizzaate, for, dinner, pasta, and, …only "and"

The overlap gives the answer: queen shares most of king's company, so it can probably stand in for king; pizza shares almost none, so it probably can't. Skip-gram does this comparison implicitly. It never lists or compares neighbours; instead, words with overlapping neighbour lists get pulled towards the same context vectors, so their target vectors end up close together.

This makes every word play two parts: something to be described (by its company) and part of the description of other words. Distributional semantics has always drawn this as a word × context table, with words being described as rows and the contexts describing them as columns. The two vectors are those two roles: w_in holds each word's row vector and w_out its column vector. Contexts don't even have to be words. Some variants use grammatical relations or whole documents, which makes clear that "being a word" and "being a context" are different jobs.

A historical footnote: word2vec didn't start from this linguistic argument. Its two matrices came from its neural-network design: an input layer (word → hidden) and an output layer (hidden → predicted word), each with its own weights. The explanation in terms of the word × context table came afterwards, notably from Levy and Goldberg (2014). Still, the reason it works matches the linguistic distinction, and so does the reason the two tables must stay separate.

Why two vectors per token?

In the codethe score: a dot productskip_gram_network.py line 92w_in pulled towards w_outskip_gram_network.py line 127w_out pulled towards w_inskip_gram_network.py line 128

1. A vector always agrees with itself. With a single table, the score for (cat, cat) would be sigmoid(v_cat · v_cat), which is always high, because a vector points the same way as itself. The model would believe every word appears next to itself, which is rarely true. With separate tables, w_in[cat] and w_out[cat] are free to differ.

2. This is how similar words become similar. In your corpus, "king" and "queen" appear in the same window only once ("the king and queen rule the kingdom"). But they appear in parallel sentences, such as "the king wore a golden crown" and "the queen wore a silver crown", so as targets they share many of the same contexts: sits, wore, crowned, became, will, and more. Training keeps pulling w_in[king] towards those context vectors in w_out, and does exactly the same to w_in[queen]. Because both are pulled towards the same points, they end up near each other.

The key idea: a target vector comes to mean "the kinds of neighbours I have". Two words with similar neighbours get similar target vectors, even if they rarely or never appear together. That is what an embedding is.

Which table is kept? At the end, w_in (the target vectors) are the embeddings. They're what get_vector reads and what gets saved. w_out was scaffolding: the reference points the target vectors were pulled towards. (They move too, since train_pair nudges both vectors, but they're not used afterwards.) Some implementations average or add the two tables instead, but keeping only w_in is the most common choice.

The starting values are small random numbers between −scale/2 and +scale/2, where scale = 0.5 / 32 = 0.015625. Here are the first six of king's 32 starting values (seed 42):

w_in[king] = [-0.00352, 0.00276, 0.00497, -0.00729, -0.00632, 0.00568, …]

Right now they mean nothing; they're just noise. Section 12 explains why they have to be random rather than all zero.

Why centred on zero, rather than only positive?

In the coderand() − 0.5skip_gram_network.py lines 79–80scale = 0.5 / dimskip_gram_network.py line 74updates that push weights negativeskip_gram_network.py lines 127–128

The key is the − 0.5 in initialize_weights: (rand() − 0.5) * scale. rand() gives a number between 0 and 1, and subtracting 0.5 shifts it to between −0.5 and +0.5, so the starting weights are spread evenly around zero. There are three reasons this matters.

1. Random vectors should start out unrelated. What matters about an embedding is its direction, and similarity is measured by the angle between vectors. With values centred on zero, random vectors in 32 dimensions are nearly at right angles to each other, so their cosine similarity is close to 0. Your run confirms this: before training, king/queen was 0.18, man/woman −0.24 and cat/dog −0.03. With positive-only values, every vector would lean towards the same diagonal direction, (1, 1, …, 1), and two random vectors would have an expected cosine similarity of about 0.75. Before seeing any data, the model would already "think" every word was similar to every other word.

2. The model should start with no opinion. With values centred on zero, positive and negative products cancel out, so every dot product starts near 0 and every score near 0.5: "no idea". With positive-only values, every dot product would be positive and every score above 0.5. The model would start biased towards "yes, these go together" for every pair, fake ones included, and training would first have to undo that.

3. Training produces negative weights anyway. Every negative sample makes grad negative, and synapse_in.weight += grad * synapse_out.weight then subtracts from the weights. Negative values are how vectors point away from each other, which is what "unrelated" and "opposite" mean geometrically, and analogies like king − man + woman rely on subtraction. You could only keep the weights positive by clipping them after every update, which would confine every vector to one corner where no two can be more than 90° apart.

What does "really appeared together" mean?

In the codepairs built from CORPUS onlyembed.py lines 545–546the windowembed.py lines 345–347real pair: label 1embed.py line 482fake pair: label 0embed.py lines 486–489

Both "together" and "really" have exact meanings, set by build_training_pairs. The sliding-window diagram in section 4 shows the mechanics; here is what it means for the guess.

Where: in the CORPUS sentences, and nowhere else. "Appeared together" means in the 107 sentences of CORPUS, after each has been split into BPE tokens. Two consequences:

  • STORIES isn't used. Pairs are built only from CORPUS, so the king sitting on the throne in the stories counts for nothing, while "the king sits on the throne" in CORPUS counts.
  • Each CORPUS entry is a separate unit. Pairs never cross from one entry to the next, so the last token of one sentence and the first of the next are never "together".

Together: within window_size tokens, in the same sentence. Your runs use window_size = 2, so a target is together with up to 2 tokens on each side. In "the kitten is tiny and cute":

targettogether with (window 2)not together
kittenthe, is, tinyand, cute
tinykitten, is, and, cutethe
  • Distance is counted in tokens, not words. "mat" is two tokens (m, at), so it takes up two positions. A word that splits into several pieces uses up more of the window, so fewer whole words fit within reach.
  • Distance within the window doesn't matter. A neighbour 1 position away and one 2 positions away count equally. (The original word2vec gives nearer words more weight by randomly shrinking the window for each target; your code doesn't.)
  • Order doesn't matter either. Every pair is generated in both directions: kitten → tiny when kitten is the target, and tiny → kitten when tiny is.
  • Repeats count again. Two tokens that are neighbours in several sentences get one pair for each time. "the" and "cat" are neighbours many times, so that pair turns up many times in the training list and gets pulled together more often. That's how frequency turns into strength.

"Really" means: from the pair list, rather than made up. During training the model never looks back at the corpus (why?). A "real" pair is simply one that came from the list build_training_pairs made (label 1). A "fake" pair is one whose second token was picked at random by sample_negative (label 0).

Fake pairs aren't always truly fake. A randomly drawn negative can be a pair that does appear together elsewhere in the corpus. If "the" is drawn as a negative for "cat", it still gets label 0, even though "the cat" is everywhere. The only check is that a negative isn't the same as this pair's real context. The model learns despite that noise: it sees (cat, the) far more often as a real pair than as a fake one, so on balance the two are pulled together.

Why does training never look back at the corpus?Going deeper
In the codetrain_pair: only two vectorsskip_gram_network.py lines 119–132one pair at a timeembed.py lines 480–489negatives drawn, never checkedembed.py lines 485–489

Corpus size is part of the answer, but not the main part. There are three reasons, plus a trade-off.

1. The pair list already contains everything training uses

Skip-gram's only question is "which tokens appear within the window of which other tokens?" Once build_training_pairs has made the list, it holds exactly that, with repeats preserved. Word order beyond the window, which sentence a pair came from, and grammar are never used. There's nothing left in the corpus for training to go back for.

2. The objective is a sum over separate pairs

Skip-gram's whole goal can be written as one big sum:

total loss = sum over every (target, context) pair of
             [ loss of the real pair + loss of its negatives ]

Each term depends only on its own two tokens and their vectors. No term asks about the sentence it came from, other pairs, or the corpus as a whole. That's deliberate, and comes from the distributional hypothesis: a word's meaning is reflected in the words that tend to appear near it. If local context is all that matters, the rest of the corpus is irrelevant by design.

A sum of separate terms is ideal for stochastic gradient descent: improve the total one piece at a time by taking a pair, nudging two vectors and moving on. That's exactly what train_pair does, and it brings big practical benefits:

  • Memory doesn't grow with the corpus. Only the current pair and the two weight tables are needed.
  • Order doesn't matter, which is why shuffling works.
  • Training can stream. Real word2vec, trained on billions of words, never builds a pair list at all. It reads through the text each epoch, makes pairs from the sliding window as it goes, trains on them and throws them away. (Your code builds the list once and reuses it, which is fine for 2,526 pairs.)
  • Training can run in parallel. Each update touches only a few vectors, so several threads can process different pairs at once. The original word2vec did this.

Any step that needed a global view, like "check this against the whole corpus", would break all four.

3. Negative sampling removed the one global step

Before word2vec, neural language models typically computed a full softmax. To score one pair, they compared the target against every word in the vocabulary, so the scores would add up to 1 as probabilities. That was the expensive global step: not a scan of the corpus, but a scan of the whole vocabulary for every single pair. Negative sampling replaced it with "the context versus k random tokens" (5 in your runs), cutting the cost per pair from proportional to the vocabulary size to a small constant. That, more than anything, is why word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013) could train on billions of words.

It's also why negatives aren't checked against the corpus, even though that would be easy here: put the 2,526 pairs in a Python set, and each check takes a moment. Negative sampling doesn't ask "is this pair true or false?". It asks the model to tell pairs from the text apart from pairs drawn from a noise distribution (tokens weighted by frequency0.75). Levy and Goldberg (2014) showed that this pushes each dot product towards a measure of how much more often two tokens co-occur than chance would predict (pointwise mutual information, minus a constant for the number of negatives). That result depends on the negatives being drawn independently of the data, so filtering them would actually skew the vectors.

The trade-off: other methods do look at the whole corpus

It's a design choice, not the only option. LSA and GloVe (Pennington et al., 2014) go through the whole corpus first and build a co-occurrence table (how often each word appears near each other word), then fit vectors to that table. GloVe's authors argued that using these global counts makes better use of the data. The two approaches turn out to be closely related: skip-gram with negative sampling is, in effect, fitting the same kind of table without ever building it.

The model works out its guess in two steps.

a) The dot product: do the vectors agree?

Multiply the two vectors element by element and add up the results. In the network this is exactly what the output neuron computes in Neuron.forward: the weighted sum of its inputs, where the inputs are the hidden neurons' values (the target's embedding) and the weights are on its incoming synapses (the candidate's context vector):

dot = t[0]×o[0] + t[1]×o[1] + … + t[31]×o[31]

b) The sigmoid: turn agreement into a probability

A dot product can be any number. sigmoid squashes it into the range 0 to 1, so it can be read as a probability:

score = sigmoid(dot) = 1 / (1 + e−dot)

At the start every vector is tiny, so every dot product is about 0 and every score is about 0.5. The model starts out guessing "no idea" for everything.

8. Step 6: The nudge (train_pair)

In the codetrain_pairskip_gram_network.py lines 94–132the nudge: both synapses updatedskip_gram_network.py lines 123–128old value read firstskip_gram_network.py line 126

This is where learning actually happens. train_pair is called once for each positive pair and once for each negative, about 15,000 times per epoch. Here's the whole thing (the comments are shortened):

def train_pair(self, target, other, label, lr):
    score = self.forward(target, other)                    # 1. guess
    grad = lr * (label - score)                             # 2. how wrong?

    synapses_from_target = self.input_layer.neurons[target].synapses_out
    synapses_to_other = self.output_layer.neurons[other].synapses_in
    # 3. nudge both vectors
    for synapse_in, synapse_out in zip(synapses_from_target, synapses_to_other):
        hidden_value = synapse_in.to_neuron.value
        synapse_in.weight += grad * synapse_out.weight
        synapse_out.weight += grad * hidden_value

    if label == 1:
        return -math.log(score + 1e-10)                      # 4. report the loss
    return -math.log(1 - score + 1e-10)

Step 1, forward, feeds the target in: each hidden neuron takes the weight on its synapse from the target's input neuron, then the output neuron for other computes sigmoid(weighted sum) in Neuron.forward. That is sigmoid(w_in[target] · w_out[other]), the score described in section 6.

This is a neural network: forward pass, loss, backward pass

In the codeforward passskip_gram_network.py lines 89–92Neuron.forwardneuron.py lines 59–64lossskip_gram_network.py lines 130–132backward pass and updateskip_gram_network.py lines 121–128

Your code doesn't just borrow the maths of a neural network; it is one, built from Neuron and Synapse objects by NeuralNetBuilder (see the network drawing in Fig. 8). train_pair does the three things every neural-network training step does:

Neural-network stepIn train_pair
Forward pass: input → hidden → outputforward sets the hidden neurons' values from the target's outgoing synapse weights (w_in[target]), then the output neuron for other runs Neuron.forward: the weighted sum of the hidden values over its incoming synapses (w_out[other]), passed through its sigmoid activation.
Loss: how wrong the output was−log(score) for a real pair, −log(1 − score) for a negative.
Backward pass (backpropagation) and gradient-descent updategrad = lr * (label − score) is the error at the output neuron, scaled by the learning rate. Then, for each hidden neuron, synapse_in.weight += grad * synapse_out.weight updates the input-side synapse, and synapse_out.weight += grad * hidden_value the output-side synapse.

Those two update lines are exactly what backpropagation gives for this network: the error at the output, times the output-side weight, is the update for the input-side synapse; the same error, times the hidden neuron's value, is the update for the output-side synapse. That's also why hidden_value is read before either weight changes: backpropagation computes both updates from the values before the step.

Two shortcuts keep it fast without changing the answer (or, in the second case, by changing the question on purpose):

  • Only the target's outgoing synapses change. With a one-hot input, every other input neuron's synapses have a gradient of exactly zero, so forward and the update loop only touch the target's 32 synapses. Skipping the rest isn't an approximation; it's the exact result, computed cheaply.
  • Only 6 output neurons are computed, the real context and 5 negatives, instead of all 286. This one is a deliberate change: negative sampling instead of a full softmax (section 9, Why this question?). It's still a network trained by gradient descent, just on a cheaper question.

What makes it unusually simple: the hidden neurons use the identity activation (they just pass the target's synapse weights on), so the network is linear apart from the sigmoid at the output. Deep networks stack many layers with non-linear activations between them. Word2vec gets away with one layer because it only needs the embeddings, and that simplicity is part of why it trains so fast.

The key line: grad = lr * (label - score)

label − score is the error: the right answer minus the guess.

Situationlabelscorelabel − scoreEffect
Real pair, guessed "no idea"10.5+0.5pull together firmly
Real pair, already confident10.95+0.05pull together a little
Fake pair, guessed "no idea"00.5−0.5push apart firmly
Fake pair, already confident00.05−0.05push apart a little

So the size of the nudge depends on how wrong the guess was, and its sign decides the direction. lr, the learning rate, scales everything down so each nudge is tiny.

What "nudge" means geometrically

The loop goes through the 32 hidden neurons. For each one, synapse_in.weight += grad * synapse_out.weight adds grad × the other vector's value to the target's embedding, so across all 32 the target's vector gains grad × other vector. If grad is positive, the target moves a little towards the other vector; if negative, a little away from it. The other vector gets the mirror-image update. That's why hidden_value is read first: it's the target's old value, so both updates use the values from before either one changed.

9. What is "loss"?

In the codeloss in train_pairskip_gram_network.py lines 130–132summed in train_epochembed.py lines 480–489averaged per pairembed.py line 491reported as EpochResultembed.py lines 574–575round_like_jsembed.py lines 493–500

First: what exactly is the model guessing?

For each pair of tokens (target, candidate), the model guesses:

What's the probability that the candidate is a real neighbour of the target, meaning it came from within window_size tokens of the target in a CORPUS sentence, rather than a random token drawn by sample_negative?

That guess is the score, a number from 0 to 1:

score = sigmoid(w_in[target] · w_out[candidate])

The right answer is the label, which the code already knows: 1 if the candidate is the real context from the pair list, 0 if it's one of the 5 random negatives. So every guess is a yes/no question with a known answer:

QuestionRight answer (label)A good guess (score)
Is "crowned" a real neighbour of "king"?yes (1)close to 1
Is "pizza" a real neighbour of "king"?no (0)close to 0

Why this question?

In the codereal neighbours: the sliding windowembed.py lines 345–347random tokens: the noise distributionembed.py lines 386–402k = negative_samples per real pairembed.py lines 485–489the guess: one dot productskip_gram_network.py line 92

Of all the questions the model could be trained to answer, why this one? Not because it's interesting in itself: nobody needs a "real neighbour or random token?" detector. The reason runs in three steps, starting from what we actually want.

1. What we want is vectors that capture meaning, so meaning has to be defined. Meaning can't be observed directly; we need a stand-in that can be measured from text. The distributional hypothesis (section 5) supplies one: a word's meaning is characterised by which words tend to appear near it. In probability terms, the meaning of a word w is its neighbour distribution: for every token c, how likely is c to appear near w? Statisticians write this P(c | w). Under that definition, two words mean similar things exactly when their neighbour distributions are similar: king and queen have nearly the same one, king and pizza very different ones. So "learn the meaning of each word" becomes, concretely, "learn each word's neighbour distribution".

2. Force that distribution through small vectors, and similarity appears. Written out in full, the neighbour distributions form a table: 286 words × 286 possible neighbours, about 82,000 probabilities. By design, the model has no place to keep that table: its only adjustable numbers are the two sets of weights, w_in and w_out (the weights on the synapses that SkipGramNetwork creates). So it has to produce every entry from a dot product of two 32-number vectors, w_in[w] · w_out[c], squeezing about 82,000 numbers into about 18,000. This isn't about storage: for 286 tokens the full table would fit easily. (At real scale it would matter: a 100,000-word vocabulary gives a table of 10 billion entries.) The restriction is deliberate, because a model that could store the table would just memorise each word's row separately, and nothing would ever reveal that king's row and queen's row are alike. That squeeze is where embeddings come from. Words with similar rows in the table (similar neighbour distributions) can only be represented cheaply by giving them similar w_in vectors. There's no room to memorise each word separately, so the model has to find the shared structure.

3. "Real neighbour, or random token?" is the cheapest question whose best answer is that distribution. There are several ways to train a model to learn P(c | w):

Question to train onWhat its best answer estimatesDrawback
"Which of all 286 tokens is the neighbour?" (full softmax)P(c | w) directly286 dot products per pair: too slow at scale
"How often do w and c co-occur?" (GloVe)the log of the co-occurrence countneeds the whole vocab × vocab table built first
"Real neighbour, or random token?" (your code)P(c | w), compared with chanceone dot product per guess

The last row rests on a mathematical fact: if a model learns to tell real neighbours apart from random tokens drawn from a known noise distribution, its best possible score isn't arbitrary. It's a fixed function of the neighbour distribution itself. A perfectly trained yes/no classifier has learned how much more likely c is to appear near w than chance would predict.

In one sentence: under the distributional hypothesis, a word's meaning is its neighbour distribution, and "is c a real neighbour of w, or a random token?" is the cheapest question whose best answer recovers that distribution (relative to chance).

So the question isn't special in itself; the quantity behind it is: how likely is c to appear near w, compared with chance? Any question whose best answer is that quantity would do; full softmax and GloVe are other routes to roughly the same place. Skip-gram picks the yes/no version because it costs one dot product per guess, needs no table built first, and can stream through any amount of text. Other useful properties follow from the choice: the right answers come free from raw text, and a graded probability tells the model how wrong it was, and so how far to move.

The maths: what the best possible score isGoing deeper

For a target w and candidate c, real neighbours come from P(c | w), and each real pair is accompanied by k random tokens drawn from the noise distribution Pnoise(c). In your code, k = 5 and Pnoise is frequency0.75, normalised (section 7). Given a pair, the probability that it's a real one is

best score = P(c | w) / ( P(c | w) + k · Pnoise(c) )

The score depends only on how P(c | w) compares with k · Pnoise(c): it's above 0.5 exactly when c is more likely as a real neighbour than as a noise draw. Since the score is sigmoid(dot), the best dot product works out to

best dot = log( P(c | w) / Pnoise(c) ) − log k

That's a log ratio: how much more often c appears near w than the noise distribution would produce it, minus a constant. This idea is called noise-contrastive estimation (Gutmann and Hyvärinen, 2010). Negative sampling is a simplified form of it (Mikolov et al., 2013). Levy and Goldberg (2014) showed that with the noise distribution set to plain word frequency, the best dot product is exactly the pointwise mutual information of w and c, minus log k.

In practice the model can't reach the best score for every pair, because 32-number vectors can't represent all 82,000 values independently (step 2). That limitation is the point: it forces words with similar neighbour distributions to share similar vectors.

Then: what loss measures

Loss is −log of the probability the model gave to the correct answer. 0 means the model was certain and right; bigger means worse. For each guess, train_pair returns:

  • for a real pair, the correct answer is "yes", which the model gave probability score, so loss = −log(score)
  • for a negative, the correct answer is "no", which the model gave probability 1 − score, so loss = −log(1 − score)

You can turn any loss back into a probability with e−loss. A loss of 0.693 means the model gave the right answer 50% (a coin flip); a loss of 0.105 means 90%; a loss of 2.3 means only 10%.

The code never uses the loss value itself to make decisions; it computes it as a report card. But the nudge is tied to it exactly: label − score is the slope of this loss with respect to the dot product, with the sign flipped. So every nudge in train_pair is a small step downhill, in whichever direction makes this loss smaller.

Why the logarithm? It's the standard way to measure probability guesses (called log loss or cross-entropy). It's gentle when you're nearly right and severe when you're confidently wrong. The + 1e-10 just stops log(0), which would be infinite.

The loss that train_skip_gram reports

train_epoch adds up the loss of every train_pair call in the epoch (one positive and up to five negatives per pair) and divides by the number of pairs. So the reported loss is roughly the total loss of six guesses.

Why the first loss is about 4.12: at the start every score is about 0.5, so every guess has a loss of about 0.693. Six guesses per pair gives 6 × 0.693 ≈ 4.16. The reported 4.124 is just under that because a few negatives get skipped, and the model already improves a little during that first epoch.

Why does everything become similar at first?

That middle phase surprises most people. Here is what's going on.

  • The learning rate is at its highest and every guess is maximally wrong, so the first epochs make big updates.
  • Every token shares a lot of the same company: almost every sentence has "the", "a" and common letter tokens around. So early on, every target vector gets pulled in roughly the same direction, towards "a token that appears in this corpus".
  • That shared direction quickly dominates. The average vector length jumps from 0.03 to 1.42 by epoch 5. When every vector is mostly the same big shared component plus small individual differences, they all point nearly the same way, so cosine similarity is close to 1 for every pair.
  • Small individual differences remain underneath. The scatter plots remove the shared component so you can see them. At epoch 5 the main split is between common and rarer words (kitten, puppy, lion, tiger, pizza and food on one side), which is about frequency, not meaning. Over the following epochs the differences grow, driven by the specific company each word keeps and by the negative samples pushing unrelated tokens apart, and the meaning-based groups appear.

Summary of refinement: each pair nudges two vectors a tiny amount. Nudges for words with similar company point in similar directions, so they add up, while random noise cancels out. Over hundreds of thousands of nudges the vectors grow longer and their directions settle into a layout where meaning shows up as geometry.

Why each one is needed

1. Random starting weights break the symmetry. Suppose every vector started at all zeros. Then every dot product is 0, every score is 0.5, and every nudge is grad × the other vector = grad × [0, 0, …] = nothing. The vectors would stay at zero forever. Even if they started equal but non-zero, tokens with the same starting point would get very similar updates. Small random values give every token a slightly different starting point to grow from. They're kept small so that no token starts with a big built-in bias.

2. Shuffling avoids order effects. Your corpus is grouped by topic (pets, then royalty, and so on). Without shuffling, each epoch would end with the same topic, and the final nudges of every epoch would always favour it. Shuffling gives every pair a fair, varying position.

3. Random negatives are a cheap stand-in for "everything else". Ideally, each real pair would push the target away from all 285 other tokens. That would be about 50 times more work. Sampling 5 at random, weighted by frequency, gives about the same effect on average for far less work. That's the "negative sampling" in "skip-gram with negative sampling".

Seeded, so it's repeatable

mulberry32 is a pseudo-random generator: a short formula that turns a starting number (the seed) into a sequence that looks random but is completely determined by the seed.

That's why running your code twice gives identical results to the last digit, and why the Python matches the TypeScript: both use the same generator with the same seed, and draw from it in exactly the same order. (That's also why shuffle uses rand() instead of Python's random.shuffle, and why initialize_weights alternates between each token's w_in and w_out synapses, value by value.)

One stream, so everything is connected. Because there's only one generator, changing anything that uses random numbers changes everything after it. For example, with negative_samples = 4 instead of 5, the second epoch's shuffle uses different random numbers, so the order is different, so every later nudge is different.

14. Where this leads: from skip-gram to next-token prediction

In the codethe dot-product scoreskip_gram_network.py line 92the −log lossskip_gram_network.py lines 130–132

The yes/no "real neighbour?" game foreshadows next-word prediction, the task behind today's large language models. Historically it went the other way round: skip-gram is a deliberately simplified version of next-word prediction, and the same ideas reappear inside modern models.

Where skip-gram came from

Before word2vec, neural language models (Bengio et al., 2003) did predict the next word: "given the previous few words, which word comes next?" They learned word embeddings along the way. Word2vec (2013) made that much cheaper with two simplifications: predict any nearby word instead of the next one, and replace "which word, out of the whole vocabulary?" with a yes/no question.

Next-word prediction (earlier models, and today's LLMs)Skip-gram (your code)
Predictsthe next tokenany neighbour within the window, either side
Uses order?yes: what came beforeno: just "nearby"
Kind of guessmultiple choice among the whole vocabularyyes/no: real neighbour or random token?
Loss−log(probability given to the actual next token)−log(probability given to the right yes/no answer)

Where it leads

Large language models such as GPT predict the next token, and skip-gram's structure is visible inside them:

  • An input embedding table, like w_in: every token starts as a learned vector.
  • An output embedding table, like w_out: to score each possible next token, the model takes a dot product with that token's output vector. That's the same move as w_in[w] · w_out[c] in train_pair. (Some models, GPT-2 for example, even reuse the input table as the output table.)
  • The same loss idea: −log of the probability given to the right answer, called cross-entropy. The difference is that the answer is "which of 50,000+ tokens comes next?" rather than yes/no, and a softmax turns the dot products into probabilities that add up to 1.

The big difference: context

In skip-gram, the dot product uses the target's embedding directly, so "bank" has one vector whether it means a river bank or a money bank. In an LLM, the embedding first passes through many transformer layers that mix in information from the surrounding words. By the time of the dot product, the vector depends on the whole sentence so far. These are contextual embeddings rather than static ones.

One reversal. For skip-gram, the guessing is a proxy and the embeddings are what you keep (section 9). For an LLM, next-token prediction is the product: generating text is just repeated next-token guessing, adding each chosen token to the input and guessing again.

So the yes/no "real neighbour?" game is a stripped-down ancestor of next-token prediction. It has the same ingredients (embedding vectors, dot-product scores, a −log-probability loss), just without order, context or a vote across the full vocabulary.
